# The CMO Is Being Carved Up, Not Replaced

The CMO title isn't disappearing. It's being dismembered, one function at a time, and handed out across the C-suite like a job nobody wanted to own all at once.

In the span of two weeks, three independent reports described the same pattern from three different angles. MediaPost ran a piece titled "The CMO Is Dead, Long Live The Chief Messaging Officer," documenting how pricing strategy is moving to a Chief Commercial Officer, analytics to a Chief Data Officer, and innovation to a newly invented Chief Brand Innovation Officer. The CMO Barometer 2026, run by Serviceplan Group, the University of St. Gallen, and Heidrick & Struggles, surveyed 805 marketing decision-makers across 15 countries and found 68% believe AI will define every part of their strategy in 2026. Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey found CMOs allocating 15.3% of budget to AI while only 30% call their AI readiness mature.

Read separately, these look like three trend pieces. Read together, they describe one thing: the marketing function is being split into pieces small enough for other executives to swallow, while AI absorbs whatever execution work is left.

Everyone treats this as evolution. I think it's the mistake that's been driving positioning failures at B2B startups for the last two years, and it's about to get worse.

Here's why fragmentation feels like progress, why it isn't, and what I'd build instead for a 20 to 200 person Israeli startup heading into a 2026 budget cycle.

---

## Three Studies, One Pattern

The MediaPost piece is the clearest map of what's happening. Maarten Albarda describes the CMO's old empire, the classic 4Ps (product, price, place, promotion), getting carved up in real time. Pricing and go-to-market strategy move to the CCO. Customer data and analytics move to the CDO or CTO. Innovation gets a title of its own. What's left for the CMO is brand and messaging, which is why the piece jokes the role should be renamed Chief Messaging Officer.

NIQ's 2026 CMO Outlook adds the budget angle. 83% of CMOs say they're confident in their brand equity, even as they face tightening budgets and sharper ROI scrutiny. NIQ calls this a "reputation and results reckoning." Confidence in the brand, no confidence in the resources to defend it.

Gartner's numbers are the sharpest warning. 56% of CMOs say their organization doesn't have the budget to deliver its 2026 strategy. Meanwhile they're pouring 15.3% of what budget they do have into AI, and only 30% think their AI capability is actually mature enough to justify that spend.

Put the three together and you get an executive function that's shrinking in headcount and authority, expanding in AI spend, and less confident than ever that the spend is working. That's not a marketing department maturing. That's a marketing department being quietly disassembled while everyone calls it modernization.

---

## Fragmentation Feels Smart. It's the Trap.

The logic behind carving up the CMO role sounds reasonable on paper. Pricing is a commercial decision, so give it to the CCO. Data is a technical asset, so give it to the CDO. Execution is now cheap and fast with AI, so let agents run campaigns, content, and customer interactions. According to Demand Gen Report, citing Juniper Research, automated customer interactions are projected to grow from 3.3 billion in 2025 to over 34 billion by 2027. Each individual handoff looks efficient.

The problem is what happens to the thing that ties all of it together: the narrative. Pricing decisions communicate value. Data decisions shape who you claim to serve. Innovation claims set expectations for what the product can do. None of that works in isolation. It only works if one person is responsible for whether the story it tells is coherent and true.

When you split those decisions across a CCO, a CDO, and a pile of AI agents, you don't get four experts collaborating on one narrative. You get four narratives running in parallel, each locally optimized, none of them owned end to end. I've seen this exact failure mode inside startups that never called it "fragmentation" because they never had four chiefs. They had a growth hacker running performance, a brand agency running messaging, and a data analyst running attribution, all reporting into different people, none of them talking to each other before a deck went out the door.

This is the part the AI conversation misses. AI collapses the cost of execution. It does not collapse the need for judgment about what to execute. In fact it raises the stakes on judgment, because AI will happily produce five different versions of your positioning at five times the speed if five different people are feeding it five different briefs. Fragmentation plus AI doesn't average out to clarity. It multiplies the confusion and ships it faster.

## The Israeli Startup Version of This Mistake

Enterprise companies fragment the CMO role by minting new titles. Israeli startups between 20 and 200 people do the same thing without the titles. Instead of a Chief Commercial Officer, they hire a sales-side growth consultant who owns messaging for outbound. Instead of a Chief Data Officer, the head of product runs analytics and quietly decides which customer segment the roadmap should chase. Instead of a Chief Brand Innovation Officer, there's a freelance brand agency retained for the pitch deck redesign, working from a brief nobody updated in eight months.

None of these people are wrong to do their jobs. The problem is structural: there's no single senior owner accountable for whether the story told by the sales deck, the product roadmap, and the brand agency actually agree with each other. I've walked into companies at Series A and Series B where the CEO could recite three different one-liners for what the company does, each one correct in its own context and none of them consistent with the others.

That inconsistency isn't a branding problem. It's a governance problem. Nobody owns the narrative end to end, so every function optimizes its own slice and calls it strategy. This is exactly what the CMO Barometer's AI finding predicts at scale: 68% of marketing leaders expect AI to touch every part of their 2026 strategy, and if positioning ownership is already fragmented, AI just means five departments generating content five times faster from five different assumptions about who the customer is.

## What I Do Differently

As a fractional CMO, my job on day one is almost never to add another specialist to the mix. It's to consolidate judgment into one seat, even if that seat is part-time. Pricing input, product positioning, brand voice, and go-to-market sequencing all route through one person who's accountable for whether the story holds together across every channel and every deal.

AI absorbs the execution underneath that seat, not around it. Drafting messaging variants, building sales collateral, running competitive research, generating campaign copy: all of that goes to AI, fast. What doesn't go to AI is the decision about which positioning is true, which segment we're actually built for, and which tradeoffs we're willing to make in the pitch. That's judgment, and judgment doesn't scale by adding more people who each own a fragment of it.

At one company I worked with, the CEO had effectively become the Chief Commercial Officer, the head of product had become the de facto Chief Data Officer, and marketing was a function that produced whatever collateral the loudest stakeholder asked for that week. The fix wasn't hiring three more executives to formalize those roles. It was pulling positioning, pricing narrative, and product messaging back under one accountable owner, and letting the existing team (with AI doing the heavy lifting on production) execute against a single brief. Sales cycle conversations got shorter within one quarter, not because the product changed, but because every conversation started from the same story.

## The 90-Day Fix Before Budgets Lock In

If you're a founder or CEO of a 20 to 200 person B2B startup heading into 2026 budget planning, the fragmentation instinct will show up in your own org chart discussions. Someone will suggest a Head of Growth, a separate brand hire, and an AI tools budget, each reporting to a different function. Resist the urge to solve a coherence problem by adding more owners.

Here's the sequence I'd run in the next 90 days instead:

- Name one senior owner of positioning and narrative, even fractional, with explicit authority over messaging, pricing story, and category claims (not the org chart, the narrative)
- Audit every place your value proposition currently lives (deck, website, sales scripts, product marketing, investor updates) and flag every inconsistency before you spend a shekel on new tools
- Route AI budget through that single owner's brief, not through separate departmental AI initiatives that each generate content against their own assumptions
- Cut any vendor or hire whose deliverable is a standalone narrative artifact (a rebrand, a messaging deck, a positioning workshop) unless it reports into the one owner accountable for coherence
- Re-test your one-liner with your own sales team before the quarter ends: if you get three different answers, you haven't fixed positioning, you've just fragmented it more quietly

---

I've built this function enough times to know the fix is never more chiefs. It's fewer owners with real authority, and AI doing the execution underneath them instead of a title being invented around them. If your 2026 planning is starting to look like a chart with five new names on it, that's usually the moment to call someone in to consolidate, not expand.
