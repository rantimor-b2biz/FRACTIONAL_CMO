# The Buying Committee Didn't Get More Complex. Your Positioning Did.

Every research report landing in my inbox this month says the same thing. Buying committees are exploding. Five stakeholders became sixteen. Deals stall in internal conflict. And the prescribed fix, from every vendor with a booth at your next conference, is the same: more personalization, more content variants, more ABM tooling to reach all sixteen people with a message tailored to each.

I think that's the wrong lesson. And I think a lot of founders are about to spend budget proving it.

A 16-person buying committee isn't a distribution problem. It's a positioning stress test. Most B2B startups I work with are smart teams, good products, real traction. And they're failing that test long before anyone opens a tool to personalize anything.

Here's the thing nobody's saying out loud. If your narrative can't survive one buyer explaining it to fifteen others without you in the room, more content variants just means more confused people, faster.

---

## The Stat Everyone's Panicking About

According to Improvado's '13 B2B Marketing Trends Defining 2026,' buying committees have expanded from an average of 5 to 16 decision-makers, and 74% of these groups experience internal conflict during the purchase process. That's not a rounding error. That's a structural shift in how enterprise deals get made.

The instinctive response from most marketing teams is to scale personalization. Build a persona-specific one-pager for the CFO. Another for the security lead. Another for the end user. Feed it all into an AI content engine and generate variants at speed, because according to the same Improvado report, 79% of B2B buyers are already using AI tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews to do their own research before anyone from your team even knows they're in market.

So the industry's answer is: more content, more personalization, more AI-assisted volume to match AI-assisted research. It sounds logical. It's also treating a symptom while ignoring the disease.

The disease is this. When a buying committee has grown to 16 people, at least 10 of them will never speak to your sales team directly. They'll hear about your product from a colleague, in a Slack message, in a five-minute hallway conversation, in a summary slide someone else built. If your positioning depends on you being in the room to clarify it, it dies the moment the committee gets larger than the room you're in.

---

## 16 Stakeholders Isn't a Personalization Problem. It's a Stress Test.

Think about what actually happens inside a 16-person buying group. Someone champions your product internally. They believe in it. They've seen the demo, read the deck, maybe even talked to your team directly.

Now that person has to go convince finance, legal, IT security, the end-user team, and three other executives who've never heard of you. They're not going to forward your deck to all fifteen. They're going to explain it, in their own words, in a meeting you're not in.

That's the moment your positioning either holds or collapses. Not the pitch. Not the deck. The retell.

I've sat in enough post-mortems on stalled enterprise deals to know the pattern by heart. The champion loved the product. The champion could not explain it well enough for the committee to align. Legal asked what problem this actually solves. Finance asked why it costs what it costs. IT asked how it's different from three other tools already in the stack. The champion didn't have crisp answers, because the answers were never crisp to begin with. They were buried in a 40-slide deck built for a sales call, not for a retell.

That's not a content gap. It's a positioning gap. And no amount of AI-generated content variants fixes a narrative that was never sharp in the first place. You're just generating more confusing content, faster, and shipping it to more people.

---

## The Elevator Retell Test

Here's the test I use with every founder I work with, before we touch a single campaign or tool. Can someone who heard your pitch once explain it accurately to a stranger, in under 60 seconds, without you correcting them afterward?

Not the tagline. Not the one-liner from the homepage. The actual value. What problem you solve, for whom, and why it matters enough to change a budget line.

Most founders fail this test. Not because they don't understand their own product. They understand it better than anyone. They fail because the way they've built their narrative depends on nuance, context, and follow-up questions that only they can answer live. That works in a demo. It falls apart in a retell.

I call it the elevator retell test, and it matters more than the deck. The deck is a controlled environment. The retell is not. The retell happens in a Slack thread, a two-line email summary, a hallway conversation between two people who've never met you. If your positioning only survives when you're the one saying it, you've built a narrative that doesn't scale past the first conversation.

The fix isn't a shorter deck. It's a narrative built to survive dilution. One that still makes sense after being compressed, paraphrased, and repeated by someone who's not a marketer and doesn't have your slides in front of them.

---

## Why 81% of Deals Are Won Before Sales Shows Up

This gets more urgent when you layer in a second data point. According to 6Sense research cited in Effiqs' 'GTM Strategy for B2B SaaS in 2026,' 81% of B2B buyers choose their vendor before ever talking to sales, and 41% start the process with a single preferred vendor already in mind.

Forrester's 2026 Buyers' Journey Survey, also cited via Effiqs, found that 94% of buyers now use generative AI as their primary research tool during the buying process.

Put those two facts together and the implication is uncomfortable for a lot of Israeli B2B startups I talk to. Your buyer is forming an opinion about you from an AI summary, a peer's Slack message, a G2 review, or a five-minute skim of your website, long before your sales team gets a call on the calendar. There is no live conversation to correct the narrative if it's confusing. There's no founder in the room to clarify. There's just whatever version of your story survived the compression.

This is exactly why positioning has become more important, not less, in an AI-saturated research environment. AI doesn't invent clarity you don't have. It amplifies whatever narrative already exists, sharp or fuzzy. If your story is fuzzy, AI-assisted research just spreads the fuzziness to more stakeholders, faster, before you ever get a chance to fix it live.

---

## The CAC Payback Proof

If you want proof this isn't theoretical, look at what's happening to cost of growth. According to Benchmarkit's 2025 data, cited by Effiqs, median CAC payback for private SaaS companies has stretched to 20-23 months, more than double the 8-10 month benchmark from five years ago.

That's not just a market-conditions story. It's a positioning story. When your narrative doesn't survive the retell, deals take longer to close, more stakeholders need convincing through additional cycles, and marketing spends more to compensate for a story that isn't doing its job unassisted.

Marketing Week's 'State of B2B Marketing 2025' adds another layer here. Over a third of B2B marketers report being in conflict with sales, and half work at companies where marketing exists to serve sales rather than shape strategy. That internal friction is often a downstream symptom of the same root cause. Nobody owns getting the narrative sharp enough to survive without a live human explaining it. Sales blames marketing for weak content. Marketing blames sales for inconsistent messaging in the field. Both are right, and both are treating a positioning failure as a departmental turf issue.

When CAC payback doubles, the instinct is to cut spend or add more automation to squeeze efficiency out of the existing motion. I'd argue the better first move is auditing whether your narrative can survive a retell at all. Fix that, and a lot of the downstream inefficiency, longer cycles, more touches needed, more internal conflict about messaging, starts resolving itself.

---

## The Fix: One Narrative, Three Proof Points, Zero Jargon

The framework I use with founders isn't complicated. It's disciplined.

Start with one sentence that names the problem you solve and for whom, without using your product category as a crutch. Not 'we're an AI-powered observability platform.' Something closer to: 'we help engineering teams find the root cause of production incidents before customers notice.' A stranger can repeat that. Nobody can repeat 'AI-powered observability platform' accurately after hearing it once.

Then back it with exactly three proof points, no more. Three is memorable. Seven is a deck. If your champion needs to remember seven differentiators to explain you to the committee, you've already lost the retell.

Then run the jargon test. Read your positioning statement out loud to someone outside your industry. If they need you to define more than one term, you've built a narrative for insiders, not for a 16-person committee where only two people are insiders.

This isn't about dumbing down a complex product. It's about respecting that most of the people who'll decide whether you get the deal will never talk to you directly. Build the narrative for them, not for the room you're currently standing in.

- One sentence: problem + who it's for, no category jargon
- Three proof points, maximum, memorable enough to survive a retell
- Jargon test: if an outsider needs more than one term defined, it's not ready
- Test it live: ask a customer or new hire to explain your product back to you cold

---

I've run this exercise with founders who were convinced their problem was a content gap or a tooling gap. Almost every time, it turned out to be the same thing: a narrative that only worked when they were the one telling it. Fixing that doesn't require a bigger martech stack. It requires sitting down, stripping the story to what survives a retell, and testing it on someone who wasn't in the room for the pitch. That's the work I do with founders before we touch a single campaign, because no amount of personalization fixes a story that can't travel on its own.
