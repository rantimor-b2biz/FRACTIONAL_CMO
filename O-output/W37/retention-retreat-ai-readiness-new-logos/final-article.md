# The Retention Retreat: Why CMOs Are Gutting Loyalty Budgets to Chase AI-Fueled New Logos, and Making Themselves Less AI-Ready in the Process

There's a board slide making the rounds right now that looks great in the meeting and terrible under scrutiny.

New logo count is up. Acquisition spend is up. The CMO points to a chart and says some version of "we're building an AI-led growth engine." The board nods. Nobody asks what got cut to pay for it.

What got cut, according to Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend survey, is retention. Loyalty and retention now account for less than 15% of overall media spend, a 29% drop, as budgets shift toward acquisition and paid digital channels (Marketing Dive, "Media spend on customer acquisition accelerates amid rush to AI: Gartner"). Paid media is now 31.4% of budgets, funded largely by cuts to agencies and retention (Gartner, "CMO Spend 2026").

Here's the part that should worry every board approving that slide: the same Gartner data shows organizations that direct a larger share of budget to loyalty and retention are more AI-mature, not less. The CMOs cutting retention to "fund AI" are, on average, the ones falling further behind on AI.

I want to walk through why that correlation exists, because once you see it, the "acquisition-first, AI-led" pitch stops looking like strategy.

---

## The Board Slide That Looks Great Until You Read the Fine Print

New logos are the easiest number in marketing to show a board. Clean count, moves fast, maps straight to a story: we're growing. Retention has no equivalent. It shows up as a churn rate that stayed flat, an NRR figure that's hard to explain in one slide. Boards fund what's easy to defend in a room.

So when a board asks a CMO to "show AI-led growth," the fastest way to comply is to redirect budget toward acquisition, layer in AI-powered targeting and content generation, and report a lift in top-of-funnel volume. It looks like progress.

What it doesn't show is CAC quietly climbing, because the same Gartner report flags that these trade-offs "may boost short-term performance but risk weakening long-term growth." And it hides the second cost: the CMO just defunded the function that was supposed to make the AI investment work.

---

## The Data Nobody's Connecting: Retention Spend Down, AI Readiness Down With It

Marketing Week's Q1 2026 CMO survey, covering 400+ senior marketers, mostly at companies with $1 billion or more in revenue, found confidence in achieving customer retention goals fell from 48% to 38% year over year. That's a ten-point drop in one year, at exactly the moment retention budgets were being cut to fund something else.

The same survey produced the stat circulating everywhere: 70% of CMOs say becoming an AI leader is critical, and the same 70% admit their internal processes aren't mature enough to scale AI. Trade press covered it as a standalone readiness gap. Almost nobody put it next to the retention numbers.

Put them side by side and a pattern appears. The CMOs telling boards "we're going AI-first" by cutting retention are, statistically, the same CMOs admitting their AI processes aren't mature enough to work. Marketing Week's own data backs this up: AI-mature companies allocate 21.3% of budget to AI initiatives against a 15.3% average, and report meaningfully higher overall marketing budgets. AI maturity doesn't come from a bigger acquisition line. It comes from a better-funded operation where retention isn't the first thing sacrificed for a growth story.

These may look like two unrelated trends. I don't think they are. The data shows correlation; the operating model explains why the relationship may be causal.

---

## Why This Isn't a Coincidence: Retention Is Where Your AI's Training Data Actually Lives

Here's the operating model. AI systems need structured, high-fidelity data to do anything useful. Propensity models, churn prediction, expansion scoring, all of it runs on customer behavior data: usage patterns, support tickets, renewal history, expansion conversations, customer success sentiment.

Acquisition generates signals. Retention generates outcomes.

The signals are real, and your models use them: which segments respond, which messages convert, which channels bring qualified pipeline, what your ICP looks like on paper. But all of it is measured before the customer has proven anything. It describes a promising prospect.

Outcomes are what happened next. Which accounts stayed. Which expanded without being pushed. Which churned quietly at month fourteen after looking healthy the whole way. That record is built by the customer success, renewal, and expansion motions your retention budget funds. AI needs both, but without customer history it can only learn what a good prospect looks like, never what a good customer looks like. Only one of those is attached to revenue that compounds.

Cut retention and you don't just lose the revenue. You lose the function that was feeding your AI the raw material it needs to get smarter. It's the marketing equivalent of underfunding R&D while doubling the sales team: the thing you're trying to accelerate has no fuel left to burn.

Deloitte research puts a commercial number on this: loyal customers spend 67% more than new ones ((un)Common Logic, "2026 CMO Priorities"). Every dollar redirected from retention to acquisition moves away from your highest-margin, highest-signal customer base and toward your least understood one. You're optimizing for the segment your AI knows least.

---

## The Israeli B2B Trap: Land and Expand Was Always the Real Growth Engine

I see this constantly with Israeli B2B startups scaling past Series A. The motion that actually built the business was land-and-expand: get a design partner in, prove value, expand the footprint, use that expansion as the case study that lands the next logo.

That motion runs entirely on retention infrastructure: customer success capacity, renewal process, expansion playbooks, and the data discipline to know which accounts are ready to grow. It's not glamorous. But it's the actual engine.

Then a board member who's seen too many "AI-native GTM" LinkedIn posts asks why new logo velocity looks flat next to competitors, and the fastest answer is to shift budget toward acquisition. The land-and-expand engine gets starved to fund a metric that looks better in a fundraising deck.

The irony is that most of these companies' actual competitive advantage, deep customer relationships, high NRR, expansion revenue that doesn't require new pipeline, is exactly the AI-ready asset the board is unknowingly asking them to dismantle.

---

## What Founders Get Wrong: Confusing AI-Led Growth With Acquisition Spend Wearing an AI Label

"AI-led growth" has been stretched to cover almost anything. Run AI-generated ad copy at more prospects, call it AI-led growth. Use an AI tool to build a target account list, call it AI-led growth. Neither requires real AI maturity. Both are acquisition tactics with a new coat of paint.

Real AI-led growth requires the unglamorous foundation: clean customer data, structured retention signals, a CS function generating the inputs your models learn from. That foundation takes longer to build, which is exactly why it keeps losing the budget fight.

The founders building durable AI capability aren't the ones with the flashiest new-logo numbers. They're the ones who protected retention spend when it wasn't the easy story to tell, because that's where the compounding advantage lives.

---

## The Fix: Fund Retention Like It's Your AI Infrastructure Budget, Because It Is

If you're a CEO or board member reading a marketing plan that trades retention budget for acquisition spend under the banner of "AI-led growth," ask one question: what customer data pipeline is funding the AI models we're betting on, and did we just cut the budget line that generates it?

The fix isn't complicated. It's just unglamorous.

Reframe retention and customer success spend as AI infrastructure, not a soft cost to trim when acquisition needs a boost. And measure AI maturity the way Marketing Week's data suggests: against overall marketing structure and budget health, not a standalone spend line you can inflate by starving everything else.

Clarity before speed applies here as much as anywhere else. You don't get an AI-led GTM motion by moving faster on acquisition. You get one by funding the part of the business that actually teaches your AI what a good customer looks like.

- Protect retention and CS budget before expanding acquisition spend, even when the board wants a faster new-logo story
- Treat customer usage, renewal, and expansion data as AI infrastructure, not a nice-to-have reporting layer
- Measure AI maturity against overall marketing structure and budget health, not against a single AI spend line
- Push back on board requests for "AI-led growth" until you can define what data pipeline is actually feeding the models

---

I've watched more than one Israeli B2B startup dismantle the exact function that made them defensible to satisfy a board's appetite for a growth story. The companies that get this right treat retention as infrastructure, not a discretionary line to trim when the pressure to "go AI-first" shows up. That's the conversation I have with founders before they touch the budget: does the AI story you're telling the board hold up against the data you'd need to make it true?
